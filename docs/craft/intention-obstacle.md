# Intention & Obstacle — the craft manual

This document defines **why anything happens** in a story: what each character is trying to do right now, and what stands in the way. Kickoff and chapter writing check every unit of story against it. The operational checklists live in `docs/know-how/story-craft.md`; this is the theory they enforce.

---

## 1 · The rule

**Every unit of story, at every altitude — story, chapter, scene, beat — must pass the one-sentence test:**

> *[Character] intends to [X], but [Y] stands in the way.*

**Intent and obstacle are specific and storyboardable**, never an aspiration or a mood. "Wants to be loved" is DNA. "Intends to ask her to the harvest dance before her family moves away" is an intent.

**I/O is not DNA.** Desires, Tendencies and Shadows are *who the character is*. Intent/Obstacle is *what moves the story*. DNA is the main source for what the intent and obstacle may be, but the two layers are never conflated.

**Don'ts are not obstacles.** A Don't is never overcome; it constrains tactics.

**The press test applies to intents too.** Not just "is the obstacle formidable" but "why would this character want this?" An intent unmotivated by DNA is a writer's flourish. (Example: a grieving poet *burning* his manuscript at a monument is dramatic but unmotivated if he craves recognition; *reading it aloud* there is dramatic, and his.)

---

## 2 · The dramatic levers

1. **Contested object** — two or more intents aimed at the same thing.
2. **Intent-as-obstacle** — one character's intent *is* another's obstacle. The tightest pairing.
3. **Intent vs. inner sabotage** — the character's own pattern blocks the intent.
4. **Shared intent, one slot** — two characters want the identical thing and only one can have it (one ticket, one job, one heart). Generates more story than six characters wanting six things.

Chapter cards name which lever each chapter pulls.

## 3 · The Sorkin canon (distilled)

**The drive shaft.** Intention and obstacle first; everything else — locations, cameos, imagery — is laundry hung on that line. Without it, it's journalism.

**Fact → story → drama.** "The queen died" is a fact. "Then the king died of grief" is a story. Drama begins only with conflict.

**Press on it.** For every I/O pair, list the easy exits and plug them — for intents (why would they?) and obstacles (why don't they just?). If $20 solves it, take the $20 away.

**Try, don't necessarily win.** The character must visibly try, every chapter. Dying in the tree is legal, as long as they die trying.

**Tree · rocks · down.** Chase them up the tree (intent declared, obstacle revealed), throw rocks (obstacles compound serially, each worse), get them down — or not.

**The gun, both directions.** Nothing pays off at the end that wasn't planted early; nothing planted goes unused. Every background cameo is a gun in a drawer.

**Stakes.** If they aren't automatically high, make the reader understand why it matters *to this character* — on the page, in what they do, not in a caption's explanation.

**The stand-in.** Exposition needs a character who knows as little as the reader.

**Inciting action early.** If it hasn't landed by chapter 2, the story is in trouble.

**Legible cold.** A reader opening any chapter must get the intent and the obstacle from the panels.

**First chapters, last chapter.** The first two hook or lose the reader; the finale is obligatory. Round the bases.

**Plans drift.** You never write exactly what you planned. That's fine; log it.

## 4 · Topology — how the strands interlock

Pick the topology first, then generate the cast's I/O lines into it. Two-handers can skip this.

| Topology | Structure | Generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Sun** | One character's intent (or absence) is everyone's obstacle | A story told around a centre of gravity |
| **Chain** | Obstacles detonate each other in sequence | Momentum; each crisis triggers the next |
| **Split-screen** | The cast divides into two locations or lanes | Contrast; cuts between worlds |
| **Arena** | One recurring event holds several incompatible intents | Maximum friction; shared sets |
| **Pilgrimage** | One distant event every intent points toward | The biggest finale |
| **Siege** | Nobody leaves; the place itself is the antagonist | Environmental and institutional obstacles; pressure-cooker interiority |

Arena and siege generate the most ongoing friction; pilgrimage generates the biggest finale.

## 5 · Fortune curves (the Vonnegut layer)

Topology says how strands interlock; the **curve** says where each character's fortune goes.

| Curve | The line | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Man in a Hole** | good → trouble → climbs out higher | the reliable default |
| **Boy Meets Girl** | gets it → loses it → fights back → wins | any goal with a setback, not only romance |
| **Cinderella** | low → gifts lift fortune → midnight crash → earned rise | the crash is obligatory |
| **From Bad to Worse** | starts anywhere → only gets worse | at most one strand per story; the one that haunts |

Rules:
- Declared **per character** in the pitch's O/I grid, and drawn as one chart with all strands, so you see at a glance what kind of story each arc is and where the lows cluster.
- **The curve dictates placement.** "Where is the midnight crash?" is a question Cinderella requires the storyboard to answer.
- **Stagger the curves.** If every strand dips in the same chapter, the story flatlines emotionally.

## 6 · The braid (ensemble mechanisms)

Ensemble strands cohere by **theme and braid**, not plot causality. The pitch declares which mechanisms it runs; generation follows the declared set.

| Mechanism | Source | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Protagonist split** | *Entourage* | Named roles: the **asset** (the still centre everyone orbits — never make them carry plot), the **surrogate** (the reader's moral compass), the **engine** (loud, reactive, generates friction), the **climber** and the **status-anxious** (the ones who genuinely change) |
| **Central question** | *Sex and the City* | One question for the story, pushed down to a question per chapter; each strand answers it differently and the reader assembles the essay |
| **Poles on one axis** | *Sex and the City* | Cast members hold maximally disagreeing stances on the theme's axis; no pole is declared right |
| **Hub scene** | *Sex and the City* ("brunch") | Once per cycle the cast cross-references each other explicitly at a shared set — the decoder ring for the braid |
| **Circulating object** | *Babel* | A thing passing between strands (a letter, a ticket, a rumour) — strands touch without forcing everyone into one room |
| **Effect before cause** | *Babel* | A consequence surfaces in one strand before its cause appears in another — manufactures the detective pull |

Hard rules that ride along:
- **Plot line + relationship line per strand.** Every character carries an I/O *and* one emotional bond in motion. The relationship line keeps a character from becoming a thesis puppet.
- **Theme is discoverable, never stated.** A character who only demonstrates the theme gets complicated or cut.
- **Coincidences spend credibility — budget them.** Strands converge only at the declared key events.
